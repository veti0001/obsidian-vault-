# Reserving Techniques — Revision Questions

## Part A — Definitions and Recall

### A1. What is the core assumption underlying the Chain Ladder method?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Past development is indicative of future development; the relative change from one evaluation point to the next for an accident year is similar to that observed in prior years at similar maturities, with stability in the internal and external environment.

### A2. When using reported claims in Chain Ladder, which consistency assumptions must hold?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Consistency in claim reporting patterns.
> - Consistency in the process for establishing case estimates.
> - Consistency in the adequacy of case reserves over time.

### A3. What are the two main approaches to handling ALAE when projecting ultimate claims?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Combine ALAE with claims, provided reporting/payment patterns are similar and differences are stable across years.
> - Consider ALAE separately when patterns differ materially or ALAE volume is large relative to claims.

### A4. What is the goal of trend adjustments in reserving?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - To adjust historical claim experience to a cost level associated with a common point in time, accounting for economic, societal, demographic, regulatory, and legal forces.

### A5. According to the Expected method, when is an a priori estimate more appropriate than observed experience?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - When estimating ultimate claims, an a priori estimate (e.g. a reasonable claim ratio/pure premium) is preferred over observed experience for the period.

### A6. How does the Bornhuetter-Ferguson method weight development and expected estimates?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - BF blends the chain ladder (development) estimate with an a priori (expected) estimate, giving more weight to the expected portion when experience is immature and more weight to development as maturity increases.

### A7. What is the key difference between Cape Cod and the Generalized Cape Cod approach?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Cape Cod derives an Expected Claim Ratio (ECR) from historical data; Generalized Cape Cod weights experience proportional to exposure and inversely proportional to development time.

### A8. What is the purpose of Berquist-Sherman adjustments?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - To adjust historical data for known changes: for paid claims, to account for changes in settlement rate; for reported claims, to account for changes in case reserve adequacy.

### A9. What is the fundamental premise of the Frequency-Severity method?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Ultimate claims are estimated by separately projecting ultimate claim counts (frequency) and ultimate severity, then combining them, with trends applied to bring to the projected cost level.

### A10. What are the core assumptions of the Closure Method?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Emergence can be forecast reasonably; claims closed at each age are a function of claims to be closed in future; average closure amounts at each age follow prior accident periods; inflation affects cost at closure; serious claims close later than minor claims.

### A11. What are the two primary approaches to projecting ceded claims in reinsurance reserving?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Project ultimate claims gross and net, deriving ceded as the difference; or project ultimate ceded claims directly.

### A12. What is the purpose of calculating "used-up" premium/exposure in Cape Cod?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - To reflect only the portion of earned premium/exposure that has been used at the valuation date when computing the Expected Claim Ratio.

### A13. Define the "ratio of paid to reported claims" triangle test and its purpose.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - It is the ratio of paid claims to reported claims by development period/accident year; used as an initial test to detect changes in settlement patterns or case reserve adequacy over time.

### A14. What is the key trade-off when selecting the time period for trend calculation?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Balancing stability (longer period) with responsiveness (shorter period); for short-tail, more weight to recent years; for long-tail, consider both short and long periods while excluding highly immature years if uncertainty is high.

### A15. When is the Expected method preferred over Chain Ladder, according to the notes?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - For new lines of business, when operational/environmental changes make history irrelevant, when early maturity factors are highly leveraged for long-tail lines, or when data is unavailable/immature.


## Part B — Explain, Compare, Advantages and Disadvantages
Source: [[1. Chain Ladder]], [[2. Expected method]], [[3. BF]], [[4. Cape Cod]], [[6. Freq-Sev]], [[Resume\Advantages and disadvantages]], [[Resume\Which method is best when assumptions change]]

### B1. Compare paid vs reported Chain Ladder: advantages and disadvantages.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Paid: advantage - changes in case estimates do not influence paid claims; disadvantage - more volatile, affected by settlement pattern changes.
> - Reported: advantage - more credible/less volatile due to higher volume, not affected by settlement pattern changes; disadvantage - affected by changes in case reserve adequacy/reporting patterns.

### B2. What are the advantages and disadvantages of the Chain Ladder method overall?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Advantages: most basic, input to many other methods, well understood.
> - Disadvantages: susceptible to changes in book of business, sensitive to highly leveraged factors for immature years, unstable for most recent diagonal, and may overfit; assumes consistent inflation and no calendar-year effects.

### B3. Explain the advantages and disadvantages of the Expected method.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Advantages: stable, simple, minimal data needed, often only viable for new products or significant changes.
> - Disadvantages: unresponsive to recent experience; does not react to changes in claim ratio (fixed a priori); errors can occur if assumptions change but a priori not updated.

### B4. How does Bornhuetter-Ferguson differ from the Expected method in responsiveness and stability?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - BF blends with development: more responsive than Expected method, but less stable. Expected method gives no weight to observed experience (most stable, least responsive). BF gives increasing weight to actual claims as years mature.

### B5. Compare BF and Cape Cod: which is more responsive and why?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Cape Cod is generally more responsive than BF because its ECR is estimated from historical data (incorporates actual experience in the a priori), while BF may rely on a selected external/actuarial ECR; Generalized Cape Cod further weights by exposure and inversely by development time.

### B6. What are the advantages and disadvantages of Cape Cod?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Advantages: produces same result with same data (less subjectivity in formula), partially responds to changes via claims in ECR calculation, more stable than Chain Ladder.
> - Disadvantages: cannot be used for new LOB (no data for ECR), highly dependent on on-leveling premiums, problematic if CDFs < 1, difficult with thin/volatile data, limited ability to use external data compared to BF in practice.

### B7. Describe the advantages and disadvantages of the Frequency-Severity method.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Advantages: explicitly incorporates inflation/trend assumptions, provides insight into claim process, can yield better estimates, helps understand internal/external influences.
> - Disadvantages: highly sensitive to trend estimates, affected by changes in claim count definition/processing, requires homogeneous mix of business, necessary data may not be available.

### B8. When is Berquist-Sherman particularly useful compared to simply switching methods?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - B-S adjusts the historical data to remove distortions (settlement rate change or case reserve adequacy change) rather than abandoning the development framework; it is a "repair" tool that allows continued use of development techniques after correcting for known changes.

### B9. Explain the trade-off between development methods, expected-based methods, and specialized methods.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Development (Chain Ladder): most responsive to recent experience, most fragile when assumptions change.
> - Expected-based (Expected, BF, Cape Cod): more stable, dampen assumption violations, less responsive to true changes in level.
> - Specialized (Freq-Sev, Closure, B-S): structural; best when inflation/trend or settlement/case changes are dominant, but require specific data/assumptions.

### B10. Why might segmenting data be preferred over any single method when mix of business changes?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - All methods are impacted if changing segments have different claim ratios or same claim ratio but different development patterns; Freq-Sev also breaks homogeneity. Segmenting isolates patterns so that assumptions hold within more homogeneous groups.

### C1. Chain Ladder — Link ratios (calculation practice)
Given the following reported claims triangle (simplified), compute simple average and volume-weighted average age-to-age factors for the 12-24 month interval. Numbers are illustrative as per instruction.
 
Accident Year | 12 months | 24 months
------------ | ----------| ----------
AY1          | $1,000    | $1,500
AY2          | $1,200    | $1,680
AY3          | $800      | $1,200

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Link ratios 12-24: AY1 = $1,500/$1,000 = $1.500$, AY2 = $1,680/$1,200 = $1.400$, AY3 = $1,200/$1,800$? Wait, wait - AY3: 800 to 1200 gives $1200/800 = 1.500$.
> - Simple average 12-24 = $(1.500 + 1.400 + 1.500)/3 = 1.467$.
> - Volume-weighted average 12-24 = $(1500 + 1680 + 1200)/(1000 + 1200 + 800) = 4380/3000 = 1.460$.

### C2. Chain Ladder — CDF selection impact
Using the factors above, if the selected 12-24 factor is $1.50$ (instead of $1.46$), estimate the impact on ultimate for an AY with reported claims at 12 months of $900$. (Qualitative + calculation)
 
> [!answer]- Answer
> - With factor $1.50$: projected to 24 months/ultimate component = $900 * 1.50 = 1350$.
> - With factor $1.46$: $900 * 1.46 = 1314$.
> - Difference = $+36$ due to selecting a higher age-to-age factor (illustrates sensitivity to factor selection).

### C3. Tail factor logic (Bondy)
If the last observed age-to-age factor is $1.05$ and no other data exists, what tail factor does Bondy imply conceptually? State advantage/disadvantage in numbers.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Bondy method: apply last age-to-age factor again as tail (conceptually extends pattern). If last factor is $1.05$, implied tail extension gives development of $5\%$.
> - Advantage: simple. Disadvantage: may underestimate tail if factors decay quickly or if last factor is $1.00$ (producing tail $1.00$).

### C4. Expected method — claim ratio calculation
Given Earned Premium (EP) = $10,000,000$ and selected expected claim ratio = $70\%$, calculate expected ultimate claims.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Expected ultimate claims = $EP * Expected\ ratio = 10,000,000 * 0.70 = 7,000,000$.
> - (No weight to observed experience under pure Expected method.)

### C5. Bornhuetter-Ferguson — blending
For an accident year, reported claims to date = $5,000,000$, age-to-ultimate CDF = $2.0$ (implying CL ultimate $10,000,000$), expected ultimate claims (a priori) = $8,000,000$, and percent reported = $1/2.0 = 50\%$.

Compute BF ultimate.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - BF ultimate = Reported to date + (Expected ultimate * (1 - % reported)) = $5,000,000 + (8,000,000 * 0.50) = 5,000,000 + 4,000,000 = 9,000,000$.
> - (Alternatively = CL ultimate * % reported + Expected * (1 - % reported) = $10,000,000*0.5 + 8,000,000*0.5 = 9,000,000$.)

### C6. Cape Cod — used-up premium logic
On-level earned premium = $12,000,000$, 12-24 age-to-ultimate factor component gives % reported = $40\%$ at 12 months. Compute used-up premium.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Used-up premium = On-level EP * % reported to date = $12,000,000 * 0.40 = 4,800,000$.
> - This used-up premium forms the basis for deriving ECR from reported claims relative to used exposure.

### C7. Frequency-Severity — combining
Projected ultimate claim count = $5,000$ claims. Projected ultimate severity (trended to projection period) = $2,400$ per claim. Compute projected ultimate claims.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Ultimate claims = Ultimate count * Ultimate severity = $5,000 * 2,400 = 12,000,000$.

### C8. Frequency-Severity — trending impact
If severity trended at $+4\%$ annually from experience period (2 years prior to projection) on a base severity of $2,220$, compute trended severity.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Trended severity = $2,220 * (1.04)^2 = 2,220 * 1.0816 \approx 2,401.15$.

### C9. Reinsurance — gross/net/ceded
If projected gross ultimate claims = $10,000,000$ and projected net ultimate claims (after reinsurance) = $8,000,000$, what is ceded ultimate?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Ceded ultimate claims = Gross ultimate - Net ultimate = $10,000,000 - 8,000,000 = 2,000,000$.

### C10. Triangle tests — interpreting ratios
If the ratio of paid-to-reported claims is decreasing steadily over recent accident years at the same maturity, what does this suggest according to the notes?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Suggests possible increase in case reserve adequacy (denominator reported higher relative to paid) or slowdown in settlement; further investigation (e.g. average case estimates, closed-to-reported ratios) is needed.

### C11. Berquist-Sherman — adjusted closed counts
Given original reported count = $1,000$, adjusted closed count = $800$ (after reflecting faster settlement), compute adjusted open count for average adjusted case estimate purposes.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Adjusted open count = Original reported count - Adjusted closed count = $1,000 - 800 = 200$.

### C12. Closure Method — basic flow
Given ultimate claim count = $6,000$, selected proportion closed at maturity $t$ = $75\%$, and trended incremental paid severity at $t$ = $1,800$, compute incremental paid claims projected at that maturity for the cohort.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Claims closed at $t$ = $6,000 * 0.75 = 4,500$. Incremental paid = $4,500 * 1,800 = 8,100,000$.


## Part D — Exam-Style Questions
Source: [[1. Chain Ladder]], [[2. Expected method]], [[3. BF]], [[4. Cape Cod]], [[5. Berquist sherman]], [[6. Freq-Sev]], [[7. Closure Method]], [[Resume\Select reserving methods]], [[Resume\Which method is best when assumptions change]]

### D1. Case 1: Change in case reserve adequacy
A company has experienced a significant increase in case reserve adequacy. The actuary has triangles of paid and reported claims. 
Questions to address:
- Which methods are most appropriate and why?
- Which methods are likely to be distorted and in which direction?
- How might Berquist-Sherman be used?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Most appropriate: Paid Chain Ladder (immune to case strength), paid BF/Cape Cod (stable). 
> - Distorted: Reported Chain Ladder will overstate IBNR/ultimate (higher reported * higher CDF). Reported BF will overstate but less severely than CL; Cape Cod reported may have larger error than BF.
> - B-S: adjust reported claims data to reflect pre-change case adequacy levels (and consider tail) to enable use of reported development after correction.

### D2. Case 2: Immature long-tail business / new LOB
The company is entering a new long-tail line with limited credible history. Early reported development factors are highly leveraged.
Recommend approach with rationale.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Preferred: Expected method (a priori claim ratio/pure premium) as most stable with minimal data. 
> - Second: Bornhuetter-Ferguson to blend expected with any early experience. 
> - Avoid: Chain Ladder (unstable recent diagonal, highly leveraged factors), Cape Cod (no data to compute ECR reliably), Freq-Sev (data insufficient). 
> - Rationale: a priori is more credible than immature observed experience in this situation.

### D3. Case 3: Settlement rate has increased
Following process improvements, claims are settling faster. Paid development patterns have changed; reported patterns appear more stable.
How should the actuary proceed?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Use reported-based methods (Reported CL/BF/Cape Cod) as reported is not affected by settlement speed changes. 
> - If paid methods must be used, apply Berquist-Sherman adjustment to paid data (fit paid vs closed counts) to account for faster settlement before selecting factors. 
> - Caution: unadjusted Paid CL will over-project ultimate losses because historical factors reflect slower settlement.

### D4. Case 4: Inflation dominates / need for insight
The book is subject to significant inflationary pressure and the actuary needs to understand drivers. Mix is relatively homogeneous and data is available.
Recommend method and justify.

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Recommended: Frequency-Severity method. 
> - Justification: allows explicit trending of frequency and severity to projection cost level, integrates inflation assumptions directly, provides insight into claim process, and can separate drivers. 
> - Watchouts: sensitive to trend estimates, requires homogeneous mix and consistent claim count definition.

### D5. Case 5: Selecting among methods when assumptions conflict
An actuary has multiple methods producing different ultimates (CL paid/reported, BF, Cape Cod). What steps should be taken to select the best estimate?

> [!answer]- Answer
> - Understand data/trends/changes: review operational/external/internal changes affecting patterns. 
> - Test assumptions: check for settlement/case adequacy changes (triangle tests), inflation, mix shifts, large claims. 
> - Evaluate method assumptions: see which hold (e.g. case adequacy stable? settlement stable? enough data for ECR?). 
> - Retrospective testing: smooth factors, test reasonability, compare to prior. 
> - Consider segmentation if mix shifted. 
> - Select based on which assumptions are most plausible; document rationale and consider range, not single method.
