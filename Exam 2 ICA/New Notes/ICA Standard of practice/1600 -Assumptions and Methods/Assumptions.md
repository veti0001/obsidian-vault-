-  Number: 1620
- Definition:
	- The actuary should identify and select each assumption that is needed for the work, except for those that are prescribed, that are mandated by law or that are stipulated by the terms of the engagement.
- Other useful info:
	- Best estimate — the actuary should select an appropriate model or data assumption for a matter as the best estimate assumption relating to that matter, modified, if appropriate, to make provision for adverse deviations. In selecting an assumption, the actuary should take account of: the circumstances affecting the work, past experience data, the relationship of past to expected future experience, anti-selection, and the relationship among matters.
	- Status quo — the appropriate assumption for a matter, other than a model or data assumption, should be continuation of the status quo, unless there is none or unless there is a reasonable expectation that it will change, and the actuary so reports.
	- "Calculation" is not a defined term; it may be as simple as adding two numbers or as complex as a scenario of financial condition testing. It does not necessarily imply that a model is used; in a model context it emphasizes the result of a model run and, to a lesser extent, model specification and implementation.
	- It may be useful, under the terms of the engagement, to report the result of two assumptions without opining on their relative appropriateness and to recommend that each user select that which meets his or her needs.
	- Model assumptions:
		- A model, simple or complex, requires model assumptions. The model depends on the purpose of the work and the sensitivity of the model run to the various matters about which assumptions could be made. There is a model assumption for each matter the actuary's model takes into account; those matters would be sufficiently comprehensive for the model reasonably to represent reality.
		- Model assumptions are quantitative assumptions about: (a) contingent events; (b) investment return and other economic matters, such as price and wage indices; and (c) numerical parameters of the environment, such as the income tax rate.
		- The actuary would strike a balance between the complexity needed to reasonably represent reality and the simplicity needed for a practical calculation. If the model specification does not take into account a matter, the result is an implicit assumption about it, usually an assumption of zero probability or of zero rate. The actuary may compensate for inaccuracies created by a simplification by altering an explicit assumption within the model if appropriate for that particular circumstance.
		- For models with interrelated model assumptions, the actuary would consider the interaction between assumptions.
		- The actuary would document the rationale for key assumptions, including the selection of input variables where significant data transformation has taken place.
	- Data assumptions, transformations or adjustments:
		- Data assumptions, transformations or adjustments are those, if any, needed to relieve insufficiency or unreliability in the data.
		- Example: where pension plan member files lack spouses' dates of birth, it may be appropriate, based on sampling or comparison with comparable data, to assume a relationship between spouse and member ages — e.g., a male spouse is born three years before the member and a female spouse three years after.
	- Assumptions other than model and data assumptions:
		- These are assumptions about the legal, economic, demographic, and social environment upon which the model and data assumptions depend. They are usually qualitative, e.g., legislation (like the Income Tax Act (Canada)), student education, the medical care system, government social security systems, and international treaties.
		- They are needed only to the extent that the model assumptions, and in some cases the data assumptions, depend on them; they are numerous and it is not practical to identify all of them.
		- Continuation of the status quo is usually the appropriate assumption here (e.g., a registered pension plan fund continues not to be taxed, or capital markets remain more or less as they are). Users may infer that assumption unless the actuary reports otherwise. The actuary would report (1) an assumption that is different from continuation of the status quo, and (2) an assumption about a matter for which there is no status quo (e.g., a student's assumed occupation after education).
	- Acceptable range:
		- There is a reasonable range of assumptions that an actuary may select for particular work and that might produce materially different results. Sometimes it is desirable that actuaries produce results within a relatively narrow range, in which case practice-specific standards may prescribe certain methods and/or assumptions to achieve that purpose.
	- Circumstances affecting the work:
		- Knowledge of the circumstances affecting the work may require consultation with the persons responsible for the functions that affect experience — for a benefits plan valuation: investments, administration, and plan provisions; for an insurer's policy liability valuation: investments, underwriting, claims, marketing, product design, policy dividends, and policy servicing.
		- An assumption about a matter would take account of the circumstances affecting the work where those circumstances affect that matter. The circumstances affecting the work are relevant for experience in most matters other than economic matters.
	- Past experience data:
		- Available and pertinent past experience data are helpful in the selection of assumptions.
		- Other things being equal, pertinent past experience data: relate to the case itself rather than to similar cases; relate to the recent past rather than the distant past; are homogeneous rather than heterogeneous; are statistically credible; and are in accordance with subsection 1440 (Data). These criteria may conflict with each other.
	- Expected future experience vs. past experience:
		- To extrapolate pertinent past experience and its trend to the near future is often, but not necessarily, appropriate. Appropriateness depends on the matter assumed — pertinent past mortality experience is a better indicator of the outlook than is pertinent past investment return experience.
		- An extrapolation would take account of a change that affects the outlook — e.g., a subsidized early retirement option may affect retirement rates; a change in an issuer's case estimate practices may affect claims development; an issuer's discontinuance of a line of business may affect expense rates allocable to remaining lines; a change in judicial practice may affect the settlement of claims.
	- Anti-selection:
		- Each assumption would normally take account of potential anti-selection — the right (or privilege arising from administration of the relationship) of one party to exercise certain options. The party may be a policyholder, a benefits plan member, a borrower, a lender, or a shareholder.
		- Examples: a pension plan member selecting a retirement date where pensions at various retirement ages are not actuarially equivalent; a policyholder renewing term life insurance at expiry for a stipulated premium; a mortgagor prepaying principal or an issuer calling a bond or redeeming a preferred share; a shareholder retracting a share.
		- In a single relationship, it is reasonable to expect the party to exercise options to the detriment of the other party if it is to the first party's advantage; but for a portfolio (policyholders or benefit plan members) it may not be reasonable to assume that every one would exercise such an option that way.
		- The extent of anti-selection depends on: the size of the advantage from each exercise (anti-selection is dampened if the advantage to each policyholder is small even when the aggregate detriment to the issuer is large); the concomitance of exercise (e.g., electing an early retirement pension may force unwanted unemployment, or an ill life insured may be unable to afford to continue a low-premium policy); the party's difficulty in making the required judgment (everyone knows his or her age, but a person may not gauge the effect of ill health on longevity); and the sophistication of the policyholder, plan member, borrower, lender, or shareholder.
	- Independently reasonable and appropriate in the aggregate:
		- The assumptions the actuary selects or for which the actuary takes responsibility, other than alternative assumptions selected for sensitivity testing, would be independently reasonable and appropriate in the aggregate.
		- The actuary would select independently reasonable assumptions — e.g., for a typical defined benefit pension plan valuation, adopt an explicit investment assumption and an explicit expense assumption rather than implicit assumptions incorporated within a net discount rate; a small defined benefit pension plan may use approximations for investment expenses.
		- Avoid independently reasonable assumptions that are inconsistent or biased in the same direction, either of which might make the assumptions unreasonable in the aggregate. If an assumption is prescribed, mandated by law or stipulated by the terms of the engagement, it would not be appropriate to compensate by modifying other assumptions; the remaining assumptions would be reasonable in the aggregate and, to the extent possible, independently reasonable.
		- Independently reasonable implies each assumption is explicitly defined, but there is no requirement to use explicit assumptions in the model specification as long as the model result produces no material error — e.g., a discount rate net of expenses may produce a value very close to one using explicit assumptions. In that case the actuary would disclose both the gross investment rate assumption and the expense assumption.
	- Stipulated or mandated assumptions:
		- Use of an assumption stipulated by the terms of the engagement is use of the work of another person (see [[Actuary's use of another person's work]]).
		- If an assumption is mandated by law and an amendment to the law is virtually definitive, it may be useful to report a result that reflects the amendment.
	- Discount rate:
		- The use of a discount rate is inherent in the actuarial present value method; it may be constant or vary over time. In selecting the best estimate discount rate, consistent with the circumstances affecting the work, the actuary may either: (a) take into account the expected investment returns of the assets that support the liabilities; or (b) reflect interest rates on relevant fixed income reference securities.
		- The actuary may assume that yields on fixed income investments at future dates either remain at the levels applicable at the calculation date or revert in the long term to expected levels. The levels of yields applicable at the calculation date would be set by reference to market yields at that date unless another approach is consistent with the circumstances affecting the work.
