-  Number: 1450
- Definition:
	- When the work involves the use of a model, the actuary should:
		- choose a model appropriate to the purpose and requirements of the work; and
		- understand any limitations in the model that might make the results of the model inappropriate for the intended purpose or might produce a misleading result.
- Other useful info:
	- Like approximation, models pervade virtually all work and affect the application of most standards.
	- Exploratory or descriptive analytics on available data, combined with the actuary's judgment, often take place before model specifications are finalized. A model's form, input variables, and calibration may be highly flexible, especially for more complex models (stochastic economic capital models, machine learning/predictive models, or other artificial intelligence techniques). When using complex models, the actuary would recognize the unique challenges in defining parameters and assumptions; consider regularly validating model outcomes against observed data to maintain accuracy and relevancy over time; and document key assumptions (model version, choice of training datasets, hyperparameters, and any data transformations).
	- The actuary would have an appropriate understanding of the model, its derivation process, and the assumptions or data used in training it (where applicable).
	- Risk classification is inherent in many actuarial models. Considerations for selecting risk characteristics with a relationship to the expected outcome include causality, objectivity, practicality, applicable law, and industry and business practices. Different risk classifications may be appropriate for different purposes; also consider anti-selection and credibility.
	- Amount of effort in validation, documentation, and risk mitigation depends primarily on the influence the model has on the decisions it supports, and to a lesser extent on the complexity of the calculations and how they are performed. Take into account model risk, the use of the work, and the benefit users would be expected to obtain from enhanced diligence.
		- Models so simple they could be performed effectively manually, or models used solely to validate other models, have such low model risk that appropriate diligence may be exercised without formal documentation or reporting.
		- Models used repeatedly from the same model specification and implementation, but with different input data and/or assumptions: diligence for choosing and validating the specification and implementation is normally done only once; per-run documentation is normally limited to noting the inputs and the version of the model used.
		- Some models require extra diligence because of greater financial significance, increased complexity, or greater uncertainty about the fit of the model to the more complex system it represents.
	- A model is appropriate and is used appropriately if: it enables the actuary to better understand a complex reality at a reasonable cost while maintaining the aspects of that reality important to the work; the model specification indicates the intended purpose can be achieved; the model implementation has been verified and understood as an accurate representation of the specification (including model training, if relevant); each model run uses input data and assumptions consistent with the specification; and each model run is interpreted as set out in the specification.
	- A standard actuarial method used within a model in its proper context is considered appropriate without further justification, e.g. the actuarial present value method for a pension valuation and the chain ladder and Bornhuetter-Ferguson methods for unpaid claims liabilities.
